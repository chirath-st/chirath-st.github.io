01 · THE PROBLEM

One recipe, one score, two open questions

The club brief was to rebuild one published model — DeepLOB — and report how accurate it is. A fair brief. But it leaves open two questions a trader would ask first:

  1. Is all of that machinery needed, or would something smaller do the same job?
  2. Does a good accuracy score turn into good trading once every trade costs money?

The brief did not ask for answers. I made them the project.

02 · WHAT I BUILT

A test bench, not a bigger model

Instead of one model, a bench that trains and scores many. Every model gets the same data and the same question: over the next 10 to 100 order-book updates, does the price go up, down or stay flat? Each run is a settings file, queued on the Amarel cluster and saved with its results; a second scorecard then charges every simulated trade its real cost.

The honest finding

A better score did not reliably mean better trading

The second scorecard turns each model’s predictions into a simple trading rule and charges half the bid–ask spread every time the position changes — the price of changing your mind. With that cost charged, the accuracy lead did not carry over consistently. In the exploratory runs, what moved the simulated result was trading less: holding longer, acting only when several horizons agreed, sitting out volatile moments.

03 · STILL A PERSON’S CALL

What a person still decides

A person decides

The bench produces numbers; it does not say which ones matter. A person still decides whether a small gain in accuracy is worth a bigger, slower model, whether a result that only looks good in simulation deserves belief, and which negative results go into the report. Here that person was me: I kept the simpler models on the table, charged the costs, and wrote up what did not flatter the biggest model.

What it removed

Removed: guesswork

Whether extra complexity pays off was measured, not assumed — same data, same question, seven models side by side.

Removed: hand-run experiments

Every run is a settings file, queued on the cluster and saved with its history — 95 runs in one format, none retyped by hand.

Removed: trusting one score

A second scorecard charges real trading costs, so a model is judged on what it would do, not only on what it gets right.

04 · HOW I BUILT IT

Five steps, in detail

1

Get the data ready

A public order-book benchmark: five Finnish stocks, ten trading days, ten price levels each side. Each example is the last 100 updates; the label is whether the price then went up, down or stayed flat.

2

Rebuild the published model

DeepLOB in PyTorch: a pattern-spotting layer, a layer that looks at several time scales at once, and a memory layer. Three classical baselines beside it: straight-line statistics, a small neural net and decision trees.

3

Try simpler and different builds

Remove the memory layer. Swap memory for attention. Drop the multi-scale block. Seven models across five horizons: 35 comparisons, all from the same settings files.

4

Train for trading, not only for accuracy

Sixty more runs with four training objectives that punish costly behaviour: weight rare moves, focus on hard cases, penalise churn, reward a smoother return. All queued on the Amarel cluster.

5

Charge the costs, then write it up

A backtest that charges half the bid–ask spread on each position change, plus filters that trade less. Then a research report with the limits stated plainly: one seed, one dataset, simulated.
